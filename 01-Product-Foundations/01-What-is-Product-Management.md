# What is Product Management?

## Overview

Product Management is the discipline of identifying customer problems, defining solutions, and guiding the development of products that create value for users and businesses.

A Product Manager acts as the bridge between:

* Customers
* Business goals
* Technology teams

The role requires understanding user needs, making strategic decisions, prioritizing opportunities, and ensuring that the product delivers meaningful value.

---

# What is a Product?

A product is something created to solve a customer's problem or satisfy a customer's need.

Products can be:

* Physical products
* Digital products
* Services
* Platforms

Examples:

| Product     | Customer Problem Solved                    |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Google Maps | Helps users navigate unfamiliar locations  |
| Uber        | Helps users find convenient transportation |
| Spotify     | Helps users discover and enjoy music       |

A product exists because there is a customer need that requires a solution.

---

# The Role of a Product Manager

A Product Manager is responsible for maximizing the value of a product by understanding:

* What problem should be solved?
* Who experiences this problem?
* Why is solving this problem important?
* What solution should be built?
* How will success be measured?

A Product Manager is not simply responsible for requesting features or managing tasks.

The PM's responsibility is to ensure that the team is solving the right problem for the right users.

---

# What a Product Manager Does NOT Do

A common misconception is:

> "A Product Manager's job is to build products."

A better understanding is:

> **A Product Manager's job is to solve customer problems.**

A PM does not build every possible feature.

Instead, they make decisions about:

* Which problems are most important.
* Which features create the most value.
* Which opportunities align with business goals.
* What should be prioritized.

---

# The Product Management Triangle

Every product decision requires balancing three important areas:

## 1. Customer Needs

Questions a PM asks:

* What problem are users experiencing?
* How painful is this problem?
* How frequently does it happen?
* How are users solving it today?

The goal is to create products that genuinely improve users' lives.

---

## 2. Business Goals

Questions a PM asks:

* Does solving this problem support the company's goals?
* Will this create business value?
* Does it improve growth, retention, revenue, or efficiency?

A product must create value for both customers and the organization.

---

## 3. Technical Feasibility

Questions a PM asks:

* Can this solution be built?
* How much time and resources will it require?
* Are there technical limitations?

A great idea is not enough. The solution must also be practical to build.

---

# The Product Manager Mindset

A strong Product Manager does not immediately jump to solutions.

Instead of asking:

> "What feature should we build?"

They ask:

> "What problem are we trying to solve?"

This prevents teams from building unnecessary features that do not create meaningful value.

---

# Product Thinking Framework

When analyzing any product decision, use this structure:

## 1. Problem

What customer problem are we solving?

Example:

Users struggle to find reliable transportation quickly.

---

## 2. Reasoning

Why is this problem important?

Example:

The problem happens frequently and current alternatives are inconvenient.

---

## 3. Solution

What should we build or improve?

Example:

Create a platform that connects users with available drivers.

---

## 4. Success Metrics

How will we know it worked?

Example:

* Increased user adoption
* Higher retention
* Improved customer satisfaction

---

# Key Lessons Learned

## 1. Products should solve problems, not just add features.

A feature is only valuable when it helps users achieve a meaningful goal.

---

## 2. Customer problems come before solutions.

Jumping directly to solutions can lead to building products users do not need.

---

## 3. Product Managers prioritize problems, not features.

Many ideas may be valuable, but resources are limited. The PM must decide what creates the greatest impact.

---

## 4. Good products balance three perspectives.

Successful products sit at the intersection of:

* Customer value
* Business value
* Technical possibility
