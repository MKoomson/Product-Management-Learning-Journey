---

# Overview

Product Managers constantly face a challenge:

> There are more valuable ideas than available resources.

Teams usually have limited:

* Engineering capacity
* Time
* Budget
* Design resources

Because of this, Product Managers must decide which opportunities create the most value and should be built first.

Prioritization helps teams avoid building features based on opinions and instead make decisions based on customer value, business impact, and available resources.

---

# What is RICE?

RICE is a prioritization framework used by Product Managers to evaluate and compare product ideas objectively.

RICE stands for:

* **Reach**
* **Impact**
* **Confidence**
* **Effort**

The framework helps answer:

> "Which feature provides the greatest value relative to the resources required?"

---

# The RICE Formula

[
\textbf{RICE Score} = \frac{Reach \times Impact \times Confidence}{Effort}
]

A higher RICE score indicates that a feature may provide greater value compared to the effort required.

---

# The Four Components of RICE

## 1. Reach

### Definition

Reach measures how many users will be affected by a feature within a specific period.

The question a Product Manager asks:

> "How many people will benefit from this?"

---

### Example

For a food delivery app:

Improving restaurant search speed:

* Affects almost every user searching for food.
* Therefore, it has high reach.

Adding custom app icons:

* Only affects users interested in personalization.
* Therefore, it has low reach.

---

# 2. Impact

### Definition

Impact measures how significantly a feature improves the customer experience or business outcome.

The question a Product Manager asks:

> "If we build this, how much better does the product become?"

---

### Example

For Netflix:

Improving video performance on slow internet:

High impact because it:

* Reduces frustration.
* Improves viewing experience.
* May increase retention.

Animated profile pictures:

Lower impact because it improves appearance but does not solve the core entertainment problem.

---

# 3. Confidence

### Definition

Confidence measures how certain the team is that the assumptions behind the feature are correct.

The question a Product Manager asks:

> "How strong is our evidence?"

---

Sources of confidence include:

* Customer interviews
* User surveys
* Product analytics
* Customer support data
* Experiments

---

### Example

Assumption:

"Users will spend more time on Netflix if buffering improves."

Evidence:

* Playback analytics
* Buffering reports
* Customer complaints

Strong evidence increases confidence.

---

# 4. Effort

### Definition

Effort estimates the resources required to build a feature.

This includes:

* Engineering time
* Design work
* Technical complexity
* Dependencies

The question a Product Manager asks:

> "How much investment does this require?"

---

A feature with high value but extremely high effort may not always be the best immediate priority.

---

# Qualitative RICE Example

Before calculating exact numbers, Product Managers often evaluate features using:

* High
* Medium
* Low

Example:

## Food Delivery App

### Feature: Improve Restaurant Search Speed

**Reach:** High

Because most users search for restaurants before ordering.

**Impact:** High

Because faster search improves the core ordering experience.

**Confidence:** High

Because user behavior and analytics show search is a critical activity.

**Effort:** Medium

Because improving performance requires engineering resources but is achievable.

---

# Quantitative RICE Example

## Music Streaming App

Two feature proposals:

---

## Feature A: Improve Music Recommendations

Reach:
50,000 users/month

Impact:
3

Confidence:
90% (0.9)

Effort:
3 person-months

Calculation:

[
\frac{50,000 \times 3 \times 0.9}{3}
]

RICE Score:

**45,000**

---

## Feature B: Custom Profile Themes

Reach:
15,000 users/month

Impact:
1

Confidence:
70% (0.7)

Effort:
2 person-months

Calculation:

[
\frac{15,000 \times 1 \times 0.7}{2}
]

RICE Score:

**5,250**

---

## Decision

Feature A should be prioritized because it:

* Reaches more users.
* Has greater impact.
* Has stronger evidence.
* Creates more value for the engineering effort.

# Case Study: Netflix Feature Prioritization

## Problem

Netflix wants to improve the viewing experience and can only prioritize one feature.

Options:

A. Skip TV show intros automatically.

B. Add animated profile avatars.

C. Improve video loading speed on slow internet.

D. Customize subtitle colors.

---

## Decision

Feature C: Improve video loading speed on slow internet.

---

## Reasoning

Streaming quality is directly connected to Netflix's core customer job:

> "Help users enjoy entertainment without interruption."

Buffering issues create frustration and may cause users to leave the platform.

Improving playback performance could:

* Increase user satisfaction.
* Improve retention.
* Encourage longer viewing sessions.

---

# Important Product Management Lessons

## 1. Prioritization is about trade-offs

Choosing one feature means choosing not to build another feature immediately.

The question is not:

> "Is this feature good?"

The question is:

> "Is this the best use of our resources right now?"

---

## 2. Data strengthens decisions

Strong Product Managers do not rely only on opinions.

They ask:

* How many users experience this problem?
* How often does it happen?
* What evidence supports this decision?
* What assumptions still need testing?

---

## 3. Features should support the core product purpose

A feature can be interesting but still not be the right priority.

The best features are those that create meaningful value for customers and the business.

# Personal Reflection

Before learning prioritization frameworks, I viewed feature decisions mainly from the perspective of whether an idea was useful or interesting.

Through RICE prioritization, I learned that Product Managers must evaluate ideas based on evidence, impact, reach, confidence, and effort.

A feature being valuable does not automatically mean it should be built first. The role of a Product Manager is to identify the opportunity that creates the greatest value with the resources available.

I also learned that good prioritization requires balancing customer needs, business goals, and technical limitations rather than relying on personal opinions.

---

# Key Takeaway

> **Product Managers do not prioritize features because they are exciting. They prioritize opportunities because they create the greatest value.**
